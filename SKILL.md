# Evidence-based estimating, lite

Build a reviewable forecast from the proposed scope and the best relevant evidence available. Keep observed facts, assumptions, and judgment distinct. Historical hours are inputs to an estimate, not a guarantee of future results.

## Define the work

Capture the objective, deliverables, quantities, acceptance criteria, constraints, dependencies, and intended team. Identify requirements for review, coordination, testing, and delivery. Break the work into packages with clear boundaries and units where practical. Ask about a missing detail when it could materially change the result; otherwise state an assumption and continue.

## Find and qualify evidence

Use the relevant records the user provides or authorizes you to access, such as completed work, time records, project notes, and prior estimates. Check what each comparable piece of work actually included, its quantity, contributors, and recorded effort. A similar title alone does not establish a match. Cite a stable ID, link, or other usable source reference where available.

For each package, select the closest useful analogs and explain important differences. Record consequential rejected analogs and why they were rejected. Note missing scope, incomplete labor capture, duplicates, and other limitations. A current estimate field is not necessarily the original forecast; verify before treating it as one. Do not invent records, evidence, or precision. If evidence is weak or absent, say so and label any provisional range as judgment-based.

## Build the forecast

For each package, start with a historical anchor where possible. Show low, base, and high person-hours and explain adjustments for differences in scope, quantity, reuse, complexity, quality, dependencies, and team experience. Label whether each adjustment comes from evidence, the user, or judgment. An anchor or low/base/high range is not automatically a statistical confidence interval.

Use contribution records to understand relevant experience when available. An assignee is not necessarily a contributor, and one person's share of a team task is not the effort needed to deliver the whole task alone. Missing history is not evidence of low capability. Do not invent individual productivity multipliers; familiarity can inform a clearly labeled judgment.

Keep shared work and bundled scope from being counted twice. Mark a package as included in another when applicable, and include shared testing, delivery, and coordination allowances only once. Reconcile the additive packages to the total, with low ≤ base ≤ high. Treat the summed low and high as planning scenarios, not guaranteed statistical bounds.

## Separate effort, schedule, and money

Person-hours are not elapsed time. If a schedule is requested, state the availability, allocation, dependencies, sequencing, parallel work, and review or waiting assumptions behind it. Adding people does not automatically reduce duration proportionally.

If a budget is requested, apply supplied rates to planned work allocations and show labor, external costs, contingency, taxes, and markup separately as applicable. Identify whether the result is cost, budget, or price. When an input is missing, show it as unknown and provide the calculation rather than inventing it.

## Present and improve the estimate

Lead with the total low / base / high effort range and its main uncertainty. Show the package breakdown, source references and analogs, key adjustments, confidence and its evidence basis, assumptions, exclusions, dependencies, and open questions. Include schedule or budget details when requested. Confidence reflects evidence quality and comparability, not merely the width of the range.

For later calibration, offer to preserve the forecast and scope before work starts, then compare them with actual effort and documented scope changes. Do not change source records or publish the estimate unless the user authorizes that action.
